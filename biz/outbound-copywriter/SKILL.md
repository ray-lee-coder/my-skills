# Outbound Copywriter

## Overview
Write cold email sequences that earn replies by combining the SPARK structural framework (Subject, Personalized opener, Agitate, Relevant value, Kick-off CTA), 7 copy archetypes matched to campaign signal, 7 power patterns layered on top, and a 4-step follow-up engine. This item comes from hundreds of B2B campaigns and applies when a known problem is being sold to a known ICP. For ICP definition, see ideal-customer-profile. For motion design, see gtm-strategy.

## When to Use
- Writing cold email campaigns for a new segment or persona
- Rebuilding underperforming sequences or A/B testing new angles
- Monthly copy refresh for long-running campaigns
- Warming up a new domain or sender with initial sequences
- Translating inbound messaging into outbound angles

## Before You Write
Gather from the user before drafting. Without these 4, push back rather than guess.

**Required:**
1. Target persona (job title, company size, industry, seniority, geography)
2. Value proposition (what you sell and the specific outcome the buyer gets)
3. Proof points (customer results with numbers, logos, case studies)
4. Campaign trigger (what signal prompted this: hiring, funding, tech adoption, event, job change, or none)

**Strongly recommended:**
5. Past campaign data (which angles worked, reply rates by variant)
6. Competitor landscape (what alternatives the buyer already knows)
7. Buyer's current state (manual, incumbent vendor, ignoring it)
8. Tone reference (paste a message that matches the voice you want)
9. Disqualifiers (who should NOT get this email)
10. Sending infrastructure (volume per day, sender count, warm-up status)

Generic input produces generic output. If you skip the required 4, stop and ask.

**Step 1: Pull campaign analytics.** If past campaigns exist, check reply rates per variant, classify recent replies, and start from highest-performing angles. Writing blind while real data sits unused is the most common copy mistake.

**Step 2: Map the competitive frame.** Every prospect has a status quo. Name it. Your email competes against "do nothing" more than against another vendor. Write knowing what you are displacing.

## The 3 Laws of Cold Email
Non-negotiable. Every email must pass all three.

1. **Relevance beats cleverness.** A boring email about the right problem beats a clever email about nothing. The first line must prove you understand their world.
2. **One job per email.** Step 1 earns the reply. Step 2 adds new value or gets feedback. Never combine jobs. Never ask for a meeting in Step 1.
3. **Earn the reply, do not demand it.** Ask a question that confirms their situation before selling. "Are you seeing this too?" beats "Let's hop on a call." Low-friction questions get 3x the reply rate of meeting requests.

## The SPARK Framework
Every cold email follows this backbone. Each element has a specific job.

**S — Subject Line.** Job: pattern interrupt, get the open without misleading. 3-5 words, all lowercase, never brackets, exclamation marks, emojis, or "Re:". Reference their world, not yours. Good: "{{company}} + outbound", "quick hiring question", "idea for {{company}}". Bad: "Exciting Opportunity for {{company}}!", "Introduction - [Your Company]", "Quick question".

**P — Personalized Opening.** Job: prove you did homework. The test: "Could I send this to 1,000 people?" If yes, it is not personalized. Good: "Saw {{company}} just opened a new office in Austin - congrats. That kind of expansion usually means [pain]." Bad: "I came across {{company}} and was impressed by your growth." That says nothing specific.

**A — Agitate the Problem.** Job: name the specific pain in the buyer's language, not yours. The test: after reading, the prospect should think "that is exactly my situation." Good: "Most teams at your stage are running outbound manually - reps spending 2-3 hours a day on prospecting. Pipeline looks thin even when the team is working hard." Bad: "Many companies struggle with sales efficiency." Vague and generic. Never agitate for more than 2-3 sentences.

**R — Relevant Value.** Job: one concrete outcome you deliver. Not features, not a product tour. One result with a number. Good: "We helped [similar company] add $2.1M in pipeline in 90 days without hiring a single new rep." Bad: "Our platform helps you automate your outbound." Rules: one proof point per email, specific numbers always, name a real company if you can.

**K — Kick-off CTA.** Job: low-friction question that earns a reply. Not a calendar link, not a meeting request. The CTA should cost the prospect less than 10 seconds to answer. Good: "Are you seeing the same thing?", "Would it be worth sharing how [similar company] solved this?", "Is this even on your radar for Q2?". Bad: "Let's hop on a quick 15-minute call", "Are you free Thursday at 2pm?", "Let me know your thoughts." Meeting requests in Step 1 are too much commitment.

**Total email length:** 60-120 words. Absolute max 150. Mobile-first. If you cannot read the full email on a phone screen without scrolling, it is too long.

### Subject Line Formulas
Write 10 subject lines before picking one. Your first idea is rarely the best.

| Formula | When to Use |
|---|---|
| `[deliverable] for {{company}}` | When you have something specific to offer |
| `{{company}} + [topic]` | General opener, works for most campaigns |
| `quick [niche] question` | When your opener is a genuine question |
| `[trigger event]` | Signal-based campaigns |
| `idea for {{company}}` | Creative Ideas archetype |
| `[peer company] mentioned you` | Referral or name-drop |
| `[metric] in [timeframe]` | Strong proof point campaigns |
| `not another [category] pitch` | Self-aware pattern interrupt |
| `have you given up on [topic]?` | No-oriented (Voss) |
| `[their problem] solved?` | Problem-aware audiences |

Always lowercase. 3-5 words max. No brackets, exclamation marks, emojis. A/B test in pairs, never more than 2 variants at once.

## The 7 Power Patterns
Layer these on top of SPARK.

**1. Question opener over statement opener.** Questions force self-assessment before deciding to reply. "Are your current suppliers meeting 100% of your inventory needs?" beats "Looks like you carry a solid catalog."

**2. Peer story over generic discovery.** Open with what a similar company told you, not "I found your company on [source]." Peer stories establish credibility, name the pain in real language, create FOMO.

**3. "Reason why" framing.** Explain why someone else came to you rather than pitching. "The reason they reached out is that most agencies charge per seat. We charge per meeting booked." Flips the frame from selling to "others are buying."

**4. Specific numbers over ranges.** "32%" sounds measured. "30-40%" sounds like a guess. "$847K" not "nearly $1M." Precision signals you actually ran the analysis.

**5. Scale credibility.** Drop a concrete scale number early. "We run outbound for 40+ B2B companies in this exact space." No adjectives needed. The number speaks.

**6. Gap-hinting CTA.** Imply the prospect might be missing something, without being condescending. "Would you be open to seeing where the gaps are in your current setup?"

**7. Risk reduction offers.** Frame offers as risk reducers, not discounts. "If we ran a free teardown of your current sequences, would you want to see the results? No pitch attached."

## The 7 Copy Archetypes
Pick ONE primary archetype per campaign. Selection is driven by signal data and your strongest value proposition.

| Archetype | Best For | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| The Observation | Visible signal (hiring, funding, leadership change) | Signal → what it implies → what peers did → "Seeing the same?" |
| The Problem-Solution | Well-understood pain with hard metrics | Name problem → quantify cost → how you solve → proof → gap-hinting CTA |
| The Referral Ceiling | Founder-led companies growing via word-of-mouth | Acknowledge growth → name the limitation → contrast with predictable alternative |
| The Creative Ideas | Prospects you can generate specific ideas for | "Spent time looking at {{company}}" → 2-3 tailored ideas → "Think any could work?" |
| The Benchmark | Data-driven buyers (VPs, Directors) | Industry data → how they likely compare → top performer behavior → offer comparison |
| The Case Study | When you have a perfect-match customer story | One-line result → before state → what changed → "sound familiar?" |
| The Whole Offer | No signal available, value prop stands alone (fallback) | One-sentence credibility → specific result with proof → relevance bridge → CTA |

Selection logic:
- Timely signal in last 30 days: Observation
- Case study for their segment: Case Study
- Data-driven buyer with benchmark data: Benchmark
- Founder-led or referral-driven: Referral Ceiling
- Can generate 2-3 specific ideas: Creative Ideas (consistently outperforms when ideas are truly specific; requires real research)
- Well-understood pain with metrics: Problem-Solution
- None of the above: Whole Offer (fallback, not first choice)

## Persuasion Layer (Chris Voss Techniques)
Each has a specific place in the sequence.

**1. Calibrated questions.** "What" and "how" questions force considered answers instead of reflexive "no." "What is your team's biggest bottleneck when ramping new reps?" Use in Step 1 CTA or Step 2 opener. Never "why" — it makes people defensive.

**2. Accusation audit.** Name the objection before they do. "You probably get a dozen of these emails a week, so I will keep this to 3 sentences." Use in Step 1 opener for over-prospected personas. One sentence only; more sounds insecure.

**3. Loss aversion.** People fear losing what they have more than gaining something new. "Most teams at your stage are losing 2-3 qualified deals per month to slow follow-up. They just do not see it in the CRM." Use in Agitate section. Real data, not manufactured urgency.

**4. "That's right" moments.** Describe their situation so accurately they think "that's right" before realizing they are agreeing. Use in Personalized Opening or Agitate. Being too generic triggers "so what" instead.

**5. No-oriented questions.** "No" feels safer than "yes." Frame your ask so "no" moves the conversation forward. Subject: "have you given up on outbound?". CTA: "Would it be a terrible idea to send over the teardown?". Use in subject lines and Step 3/4 CTAs. Avoid passive-aggressive framing.

## The 4-Step Follow-Up Sequence
Each step has ONE job. Never "just bumping this." Every step adds new information or reframes.

**Step 1 - The Opener (Day 0).** Use SPARK. Select one archetype. Under 120 words. Job: earn the reply, prove relevance, ask one question.

**Step 2 - The Value-Add (Day 3-4).** Do NOT reference Step 1. Write as if Step 1 never happened. New angle, new value. End with a P.S. social-proof line with a specific metric — the P.S. gets read even when the body is skimmed. Example: "Put together a quick breakdown of how [peer company] restructured their outbound last quarter. Went from 12 meetings/month to 41 without adding headcount. Would a copy be useful, or is outbound already working well for you? P.S. They were using the same stack you are."

**Step 3 - The Reframe (Day 7-10).** Completely different angle. If Steps 1-2 were about the problem, Step 3 is about curiosity or micro-commitment. Offer a 2-min video, one-page teardown, or single data point. Example: "One thing I keep hearing from [industry] VPs: the bottleneck is not lead volume, it is reply quality. We built a 2-min video showing how to filter for buyer intent before the first email. Worth watching, or is this not a priority right now?"

**Step 4 - The Breakup (Day 14-21).** Give a clean exit. Breakup emails convert at the highest rate — 15-25% of total positive replies come from this step. Numbered options format: "1. You are all set - not something you need help with 2. Timing is off - circle back in a few months 3. Wrong person - point me to the right one? No worries either way." Numbered options lower the cognitive cost of replying.

**Optional Step 5 - The Long Game (Day 30-60).** Re-engage with genuinely new value. Only send if you have something real (new case study, benchmark report, industry insight). Never rephrase Steps 1-4.

## Voice and Tone
Sound like a knowledgeable peer who spent 5 minutes on homework. Not a marketer, not a robot, not a desperate SDR.

- Short sentences. 6-15 words each.
- Every sentence gets its own paragraph in the body.
- Contractions always ("you're" not "you are").
- Plain text only. No bold, italics, or HTML.
- No links or bullet points in Step 1. Links trigger spam filters. Bullet points look like marketing.
- P.S. lines allowed starting Step 2. Prime real estate for social proof.
- Read aloud. If it sounds like marketing copy, rewrite. If it sounds like something you would say to someone at a bar, ship it.

**Banned words (never use):** leverage, utilize, streamline, comprehensive, robust, innovative, cutting-edge, game-changing, revolutionary, disruptive, synergy, best-in-class, world-class, next-generation, solution (say "system" or "approach"), excited to, passionate about, thrilled, reimagine, transform (say "change" or "fix"), empower, elevate, optimize (say "improve"), drive results (say what results), thought leader.

## Copy QA Checklist
Every email must pass all items before sending.

**Structure:** Subject line 3-5 words, lowercase, no special characters. Total word count 60-120 (150 max). First line specific to the prospect (not sendable to 1,000 people). Exactly one CTA, and it is a question. CTA is NOT a meeting request in Steps 1-3. Every sentence on its own paragraph.

**Content quality:** Every sentence passes the "so what?" test. 3:1 prospect-focused vs self-focused language (count "you/your" vs "we/our/I"). At least 2 power patterns applied. The prospect would think "that is exactly my situation" after reading. No banned words. No adjectives doing the work numbers should do.

**Technical:** All merge variables resolve. No broken variable syntax. Mobile preview clean. No links in Step 1. No images or HTML. No spam trigger words (free, guarantee, act now, limited time, exclusive offer).

**Proof points:** Every metric is real and approved. Named companies have given permission or are public case studies. Numbers specific, not ranges.

## A/B Testing Protocol
Do not test randomly.

1. Subject line (highest impact, easiest; always start here)
2. Opening line (personalized vs peer story vs question opener)
3. CTA type (gap-hinting vs no-oriented vs direct offer)
4. Archetype (Problem-Solution vs Observation vs Creative Ideas)
5. Proof point (different customer story or metric)
6. Length (80 words vs 120 words)

Change ONE variable at a time. Minimum 200 sends per variant. Run 5+ business days. Winner = higher positive reply rate, not open rate. Opens are unreliable. Document every test result.

## Performance Thresholds
| Metric | Strong | Acceptable | Kill and Rewrite |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positive reply rate | >3% | 1-3% | <1% |
| Total reply rate | >8% | 3-8% | <3% |
| Open rate | >60% | 40-60% | <40% (infrastructure, not copy) |
| Bounce rate | <2% | 2-3% | >3% (list quality) |
| Unsubscribe rate | <0.5% | 0.5-1% | >1% (targeting or frequency) |

Diagnostic logic:
- Open low + reply low: deliverability problem. Check DNS, warm-up, volume before rewriting.
- Open fine + reply low: copy problem. Rewrite with a different archetype.
- Open fine + reply fine + all replies negative: targeting problem. Your list is wrong, not your copy.
- High positive replies + no meetings booked: handoff problem. SDR follow-up is dropping the ball.

## Tips
- Copy that works for SMB founders rarely works for enterprise VPs. Always segment by company size AND persona seniority.
- The P.S. is prime real estate for social proof. Always include one in Step 2+ with a specific metric.
- When a campaign underperforms, check deliverability BEFORE rewriting copy. Wasted rewrites are the #1 time sink in outbound.
- The Creative Ideas archetype consistently outperforms when the ideas are truly specific. It requires real research per account. That is why most teams skip it and why it works when they do not.
- For campaigns targeting the same persona across different products, reuse structure, never reuse content. Same skeleton, new flesh.
- Write your worst email first. Get the bad ideas out. Then rewrite. Your second draft is almost always better than agonizing over the first.
- If you find yourself writing more than 150 words, you are trying to say two things. Split it into two emails.
